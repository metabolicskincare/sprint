# The Reading Experiment

Most people read at somewhere between two hundred and three hundred words a
minute. The limit isn't how fast the brain can recognise a word — it's the eye.
Reading a page means a constant sequence of small jumps, called saccades, and
short stops, called fixations. The jumps carry no information at all. They are
pure overhead.

Rapid serial visual presentation removes the jumps. Instead of moving your eyes
across a line, the words come to you, one at a time, at a fixed point on the
screen. Nothing moves. There is no line to track, no margin to return to, and no
place to lose your spot.

There's a catch, though: a word is not recognised from its centre. Studies of
eye movement put the optimal viewing position slightly left of the middle,
around the second letter of a short word and the third or fourth of a long one.
Pin *that* letter in place, rather than the middle of the word, and the text
stops shifting under your gaze.

Two things make high speeds bearable. The first is punctuation: a comma, a full
stop, or a paragraph break each earn a slightly longer hold, which gives the
sentence somewhere to land. The second is practice. Three hundred words a minute
feels brisk on the first day and slow within a week.
